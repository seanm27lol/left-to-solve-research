The higher-rank eigenvalue inequality

Statement. Let A≽0A\succeq0 be real symmetric of size nn and D=diag(A)D=\operatorname{diag}(A) the diagonal matrix of its diagonal entries. Write H=A−kDH=A-kD, with eigenvalues λ1≥⋯≥λn\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n, where 1≤k≤n1\le k\le n is integral. The source asks specifically about extending this to m>1m>1. Its author and another commenter reported having a proof for m=1m=1; neither posted a proof for the extension in that thread. The following is a proof developed for this search. Global priority and independent expert review are undetermined.

Elementary route for the original question. For any rank-d=n−k+1d=n-k+1 orthogonal projection P=UUTP=UU^T, let uiTu_i^T be the rows of the isometry UU. Delete any zero rows. OEIS A401673 — the last two entries of the domicule-tiling triangle

On a 2n by 2n board, match cells that share a side or corner; crossing diagonal matches are permitted. T(n,k) counts matchings with exactly 2k diagonal pieces. Dawid Ryłko (November 2026) conjectured T(n,n²)=1 and T(n,n²−1)=n²(n+1). The first identity includes n=0; the second has n≥1.

Every tile other than a vertical tile crosses between adjacent columns. If no vertical tiles occur, every cell of column 1 must be matched across to column 2, consuming it. Repeat to split the matching into n independent two-column strips. A strip matching gives a permutation of the 2n rows with displacement at most one. Such permutations consist exactly of fixed points and disjoint adjacent transpositions: the first unaccounted row i either maps to i, or maps to i+1, forcing i+1 to map to i by surjectivity. A fixed point gives one horizontal tile; a transposition gives two (crossing) diagonal tiles.

With all tiles diagonal, every strip uses only transpositions and has a unique perfect matching. With exactly two side tiles, they cannot be one horizontal and one vertical: all but the horizontal consume one odd-row cell and one even-row cell, while that horizontal would consume two cells of the same row parity. If both are horizontal, both lie in one strip since the number of fixed points of each 2n-row permutation of this type is even. Choose the strip (n ways) and the positions of its two singletons among the n+1 blocks comprising two singletons and n−1 transpositions. The other strips are forced. Rotation counts the same number with both vertical, giving 2n binom(n+1,2)=n²(n+1). For n=1 the answer is 2, the two ordinary domino tilings. Independent checks: original transfer-matrix rows to n=10; extension to 2r by 2c gives c binom(r+1,2)+r binom(c+1,2).

For n≥3 let L=2^(n−2), b_k=floor(3^k/2^n) mod 2. In F₂[X], A037097 encodes the polynomial H for the first 2L bits; A037096 encodes P for the first 4L. The original claims are divisibility by (1+X)^(L−1) and (1+X)^(3L−1), respectively. We get exact multiplicities in both.

Induction gives 3^L≡1+2^n modulo 2^(n+1). Start with 3²=9 for n=3. Squaring the congruence doubles the exponent and replaces n by n+1, since the square of the perturbation is 0 modulo 2^(n+2). Erdős 488 — How much can the density of a set of multiples grow?

Live catalog (kind open): https://lefttosolve.com/p/Erdos488.erdos_488 . Original: https://www.erdosproblems.com/488 . Declan Gessel’s September counterexample: https://gist.github.com/declangessel/d8e15e5ff1b6c7e99e3d86d7c66f1d08 . Alicien B.’s second dyadic-band proof: https://aixiv.online/pdf/2611.02020v2 . This is an independently reconstructed generalization of the smooth-band method, and the unboundedness conclusion already appears in prior work.

Exact target. For finite nonempty A of integers at least two, write B(y) for the number of positive integers ≤y divisible by a member of A. Is B(m)/m < 2 B(n)/n for all m>n≥max A? No. Indeed, the quotient of the two densities is unbounded across such choices, even for primitive A.

Proof. Let P=2^L with L≥2. Write H(t) for the number of odd P-smooth positive integers ≤t and Q for the product of the odd primes ≤P. With r the number of such primes, H(P^j) ≤ (1+Lj)^r by encoding the prime exponents. Consequently, for some j≥1, H(P^{j+1}) ≤ 2H(P^j), since otherwise H(P^j) would grow at least exponentially in j. Set X=P^j, n=PX, and let A consist of all P-smooth integers in [X,2X). It is nonempty (X is included) and primitive. An integer counted by B(n) equals ac for a∈A and c≤P, so is P-smooth. Its odd part is at most n, and a given odd part occurs with at most L+1 different 2-adic valuations in [X,PX]. Thus B(n) ≤ 2(L+1)H(X).

For each odd P-smooth w≤X choose the unique power-of-two multiple a_w of w in [X,2X). Take m=(2n)!Q. Among its multiples a_w u≤m, exactly m φ(Q)/(a_w Q) have gcd(u,Q)=1, because Q divides m/a_w. The classes for distinct w are disjoint: w is recovered as the product of the full prime powers of the odd primes ≤P in a_w u. Since a_w<2X, B(m)/m>H(X)φ(Q)/(2XQ). The density ratio therefore exceeds P φ(Q)/[4(L+1)Q]. The prime product φ(Q)/Q is at least the product of 1−1/j over all odd j from 3 to P−1. For j=2i+1, (2i/(2i+1))²≥(2i−1)/(2i+1), and the latter factors telescope. Thus φ(Q)/Q≥1/√(P−1). The ratio exceeds √P/[4(L+1)]. For L=16 this exceeds 256/68>2, and it tends to infinity with L. This gives the actual negation with all integer endpoints and positive denominators.

Second route and obstruction. For one generator a, floor(n/a)>n/(2a) whenever n≥a. Arbitrarily large sets are essential to this proof: simply adding such individual bounds double-counts overlaps among the multiples. The smooth cofactor construction arranges many overlaps at n and an explicit disjoint collection at m. A direct inclusion-exclusion computation using lcm(A) is exact for any fixed input, but cannot turn checking small generators into a universal statement.

Two reflection-axis identities for binary necklaces

Exact conclusions. The number of length-2n binary necklaces admitting a cut that reads as a palindrome is 2^{n−1}+2^{(u−1)/2}. The number invariant under a reflection but admitting no palindromic cut is 2^n−2^{(u−1)/2}.

Proof. Index sites by residues mod 2n. All reflections have the form j↦c−j; even c gives an axis through vertices, odd c through edges. A palindromic cut means that some edge-axis fixes the word. For a necklace of least period d, its distinct rotational representatives correspond to shifts k modulo d. If one representative admits an axis c, the shifts fixed by a prescribed axis e solve 2k≡e−c (mod d), up to reversing the sign of k, and the count is unchanged by that sign. All axes fixing the same representative coincide modulo d, since composing two gives a period. For even d a reflective orbit therefore contributes exactly two words to a fixed axis of its own parity and zero to a fixed other-parity axis. For odd d it contributes exactly one to each. The total O of reflective necklaces with odd d divides into the same necklaces as those of length u, by repeating or restricting a period block: d|2n odd iff d|u. In length u each contributes exactly one word fixed by a prescribed reflection, whose (u+1)/2 coordinate-orbits give O=2^{(u+1)/2}. A prescribed edge-axis on 2n sites fixes 2^n words and a prescribed vertex-axis fixes 2^{n+1}. Thus, writing E and V for even-period orbits of the two types, 2E+O=2^n and 2V+O=2^{n+1}. Palindromic cuts are counted by E+O; reflection without any palindromic cut is counted by V. Substitution gives the assertions. This argument includes n=1 and constant words. It extends to q colors by replacing base 2 in the word counts by q while retaining the divisor 2 from rotating representatives. Original https://oeis.org/A182084 gives offset 2, first value 5, and proposes the minimum 3n−n/p, with p the smallest prime factor of n. Taken literally this fails at n=2. A finite connected 2-regular graph is a cycle, and the only one with girth four is the bipartite C₄. For every n≥3 the exact claim holds. Structural lower bound. Suppose G is an n-regular graph of order N satisfying the hypotheses. It is triangle-free, and diameter two makes it maximal triangle-free since adding any missing edge closes a common-neighbor triangle. If N≥3n the lower bound is immediate. Otherwise its minimum degree exceeds N/3. By Brandt–Thomassé’s classification, G is a proper blow-up of an Andrásfai or Vega graph J. Written on the Wall II, Conjecture 372

For a tree T of order greater than two and vertex-disjoint path cover number p≥2 (singleton paths allowed), the conjecture states τ ≤ γ_t(p−1), where τ is the minimum vertex cover size and γ_t the minimum total dominating set size. Theorem. If p≥2, then τ≤γ_t+p−2. For p=1, τ≤γ_t. Consequently all three catalog formulations hold, counting this as one mathematical problem.

Proof. Let n be the order and D a minimum total dominating set; write d=|D|. A minimum path cover consists of p disjoint paths whose contraction is a tree, with p−1 edges between paths. Every vertex is adjacent to D. Summing degrees over D, contributors internal to their own path give at most two each, and the edges between paths give at most two each. By König’s theorem τ is the maximum size of a matching, hence 2τ≤n. Thus

2τ ≤ n ≤ sum over v∈D of deg(v) ≤ 2d+2(p−1).

When p=1 this suffices. For p≥2 it remains to exclude equality at both ends. Suppose equality holds. The tree has a perfect matching. Every vertex has exactly one neighbor in D. Every vertex of D is internal to its own path. Both endpoints of every edge between paths belong to D.

Choose a leaf path P of the contracted tree, and call its unique attachment vertex u. It belongs to D and has its unique D-neighbor across the attachment edge. All other vertices of P have exactly one D-neighbor on P. The neighborhoods within P of the vertices of D∩P therefore partition P−{u} into pairs, each consisting of the two positions on either side of its center. Thus P has odd order. Its perfect matching in T must use the attachment edge; the two segments of P−{u} must both have even order. Number the positions 1,…,m. It follows that u is at an odd position.

The odd positions to the left of u are partitioned into consecutive pairs of odd positions by these same neighborhoods: a pair cannot jump over the missing odd position u. There are consequently an even number of odd positions to its left, so u≡1 mod 4. All even positions of P must also be partitioned into consecutive pairs. The pairing is forced successively from the left: (2,4),(6,8), and so forth. Every odd center in D∩P must therefore occur at an index ≡3 mod 4. This contradicts u∈D. Equality is impossible and integrality gives τ≤d+p−2. Finally d+p−2≤d(p−1) because (d−1)(p−2)≥0. □

Partial result. Let D_m(x)=2T_m(x/2), the Dickson polynomial with parameter 1. The question asks whether ∑{x=0}^{n−1}D{n−1}(x)≡−1 (mod n) is equivalent to primality. For p odd and m≥1, in F_p one has

∑_{x∈F_p} D_m(x) = −[p−1 divides m] + [p+1 divides m].

Indeed let A=F_p^× and B={z∈F_{p²}^×:z^{p+1}=1}. Each x∈F_p has precisely two preimages under z↦z+z⁻¹ from the disjoint union of these groups: the roots of z²−xz+1 are either both in A, or nonrational and interchanged by Frobenius and in B. A repeated root z=±1 appears once in each group. Since D_m(z+z⁻¹)=z^m+z⁻ᵐ, and inversion permutes both groups, the desired sum is the sum of z^m over both cyclic groups. The cyclic sum equals its group order when that order divides m and zero otherwise. Their orders are p−1 and p+1, giving the identity.

Write n=pq and m=n−1. Since D_m has integer coefficients (use D_0=2, D_1=x, D_{m+1}=xD_m−D_{m−1}), the n-term sum modulo p equals q times the p-term sum. q≡ε_p (mod p(p−ε_p))  and  p+ε_p does not divide n−1.

Here p≡ε_p (mod p−ε_p), so p−ε_p divides n−1 iff q≡ε_p (mod p−ε_p). Each odd prime passes by taking q=1 and ε_p=1.
